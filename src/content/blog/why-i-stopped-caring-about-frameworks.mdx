Early in my career, I spent hours debating whether to use Angular or React. Whether .NET was better than Node. Whether SQL Server was the right choice over PostgreSQL.

Ten years later, I've used all of them in production. And the thing I've learned is that the framework debate is the wrong conversation.

## What actually matters

The systems I've been proudest of weren't defined by their tech stack. They were defined by how clearly the team understood the problem, how cleanly the boundaries were drawn between services, and how quickly we could change direction when the requirements shifted.

At LexisNexis, the decision to move from a monolith to microservices wasn't about adopting a trendy architecture. It was about the fact that six teams were stepping on each other's deployments. The architecture was the solution to a people problem, not a technology one.

## The stack is a tool, not an identity

I've seen engineers define themselves by their stack — "I'm a React developer" or "I'm a .NET developer." But the most impactful engineers I've worked with defined themselves by what they could solve.

When the system needs sub-millisecond caching, I reach for Redis. When the frontend needs reactive state management, I use whatever the team already knows. When the database is the bottleneck, I optimize the queries before I consider changing the database.

## The real skill

The real skill isn't knowing a framework. It's knowing when to use which tool, understanding the trade-offs, and being honest about what you don't know.

That's what I look for when I mentor engineers. Not whether they know the latest API — but whether they can reason about why one approach is better than another for this specific situation.

The framework will change. The thinking won't.
